## 文章

**Deadly super-resistant fungus poses "escalating threat"**

Scientists have found a fungus no standard drug can treat – and there are likely more out there.

A new strain of a dangerous fungus that resists every known antifungal drug has been discovered – and experts say there may be more out there.

This highly resistant form of *Candida auris* was found in a 28-year-old woman in Kenya who developed an infected wound. Although doctors tried at least 13 different antimicrobial drugs, her infection persisted, leaving doctors scrambling for answers. The outcome of the case has yet not been made public.

*C. auris* itself isn't new. It was first identified in the ear of a Japanese patient in 2009 and has since spread through 40 countries across six continents, with some strains evolving resistance to even frontline drugs.

The first known case of super-resistant *C. auris* was reported in 2022 in a 70-year-old woman in Singapore, who was found to be infected after a multi-organ transplant. It remains unclear whether the two cases arose independently, or whether the Kenyan infection descends from the first case and was imported into the country.

The findings on the new strain, published recently in *Scientific Reports*, signal a "paradigm shift in untreatable fungal infections", according to the report's authors, who are calling for a robust approach to detecting and treating fungal infections in future.

Jon Stone, infectious disease epidemiologist at Imperial College London, said the Kenyan case appears "to be the only infection of its kind". "The real figure is almost certainly higher: we just don't have the capacity to look for it," he told BBC Science Focus.

Unlike black mould or mushrooms, *C. auris* is a yeast – a microscopic, single-celled fungus that's invisible to the naked eye. People can carry it on their skin or in their gut without any symptoms, spreading it onto bedrails, door handles and medical equipment. *C. auris* is "unusually good" at forming biofilms on surfaces, said Rhodes, making it hard for immune cells and drugs to reach it once established. Broad-spectrum antibiotics can also wipe out bacteria that would otherwise keep *C. auris* in check. This makes intensive care patients – who are often immunocompromised and reliant on invasive lines and fluids – particularly vulnerable.

"If it becomes invasive – for example, when it's in the bloodstream – this is life-threatening and always needs treatment," said Stone.

**WHO IS AT RISK?** Drug-resistant fungal infections are "mainly confined to hospital patients, who are often very sick already," said Johanna Rhodes, infectious diseases consultant and assistant professor of epidemiology at Imperial College London. While these infections are "reason enough to worry", he said, they are largely a "hospital problem" affecting people going about their daily business. He cautioned against blowing this up, saying that while *C. auris* isn't really a problem for healthy people, very sick patients "very much are" at risk. This is because *C. auris* needs a point of entry into the body before it can start causing harm. "*C. auris* lives on the skin, and intact skin is a great barrier, so it can sit there for months doing no harm at all. However, intravenous lines and urinary catheters can give the fungus a 'direct access' to the bloodstream or bladder," according to Fisher.

**IS IT MORE CONTAGIOUS?** No, being drug-resistant doesn't mean that *C. auris* is any better at spreading from person to person, according to Fisher. "Drug resistance and transmissibility are largely independent traits", he said. But resistance can still help the spread indirectly, because a fungus that survives treatment simply buys itself more time to spread. "It is already a super-resistant strain," said Fisher. That staying power extends beyond a hospital: it's "very hard work" to eradicate, Fisher added, as it can survive on surfaces and tolerate some common disinfectants.

**HOW TO FIGHT IT** Even without effective drugs, there are defences. *C. auris* has previously been controlled with rigorous hygiene measures such as screening contacts, isolating cases and using the right disinfectant, the WHO notes. And even when the fungus is drug-resistant, this doesn't necessarily mean that a patient's treatment options have run out. She cautioned that more studies would be needed to confirm this in practice. Even without effective antifungal medication, doctors have other ways of fighting the fungus, said Stone. These include removing contaminated medical devices such as intravenous lines, cutting away infected tissue and trying combinations of antifungals that might work together. But all of these options are "only useful if we know when and where *C. auris* is present in the first place", said Stone. "Detection is the real bottleneck," he said. "*C. auris* isn't routinely monitored – partly because older biochemical identification systems, and a great many laboratories worldwide can't identify yeasts at all."

Even when the fungus does cause an infection, the signs – typically a fever that won't respond to antibiotics – look similar to bacterial infections. Confirming it means swabbing the patient and running a lab test; the results can then take several weeks to come through.

He is keen to stress that although the super-resistant *C. auris* found in Kenya may be the second-ever documented case of its kind, and appears to be resistant to all antifungal drugs, this doesn't mean the second case is cause for panic. "Don't forget the possibility of an explosive outbreak – or silent endemicity. For every outbreak you see, there are 100 more silently colonised patients – so you only see the outbreak if you're looking for it," he said.
